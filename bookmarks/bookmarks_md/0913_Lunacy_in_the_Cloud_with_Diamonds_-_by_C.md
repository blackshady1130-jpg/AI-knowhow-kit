While I am old, it is not so bad that I grew up in the glory days of the Fab Four from Liverpool. While my father was listening to Dixie Jazz, he picked up the habit while serving as a soldier stationed in the US (our countries were closer then, and we provided our allies with the needed protection), my mother wore out the cheap record player we had with Beatles songs.

It was in the days with matching suits, hairdo, and “She Loves You, yeah, yeah”. Very unlikely to scare your grandmother. I was never a Beatles fan, but it was part of the musical soup I grew up in.

The lyrics were really the only exposure I had to English in my early years, a language that today is so deep in me that I begin to dream in English after spending a couple of days in England. While my English is good, I am still far from being bilingual. That is in a class of its own, as those of my readers with parents who speak different native tongues will know.

I began formal English education at 12, but then it was too late. My English language and my writing would forever be slightly unhinged from reality.

The Beatles’ story also resonates with me as I have learned to accept that everything goes from orderly to complex over time. When you have learned to manage the new complexity and begin to view it as orderly, it descends into new complexity. The only option for me was to make complexity my friend and welcome the new disorder.

From the cellars of Hamburg, the Beatles became idols and then a global phenomenon that both imprisoned and liberated them, personally and artistically.

The rest of their career together would be an attempt to escape that prison, and maybe why Paul and Wings made “Band on the Run”.

While I was still a kid when the White Album was made, it was a piece of plastic that had a profound impact on me over the years, and it kept revealing deeper layers of meaning, something John mocked in “Glass Onion” while pretending there was no meaning.

The Beatles had begun their moustachioed, spiritually curious journey into a passive-aggressive, brilliant studio-wizard band, but the White Album exposed the insanity.

It is a marvellous product of deep disagreement and mutual dislike. Every song is a battle between John and Paul, while George is desperately trying to become one of them or Eric Clapton, while Ringo is trying to ignore them all, eating a biscuit.

The album is messy and chaotic, and it sounds like it was made by 30 different bands.

It did not come as a complete surprise, Sgt. Pepper and the Lonely Hearts Band was a warning about what was about to come.

While John denied that **L**ucy in the **S**ky with **D**iamonds was a reference to a psychedelic drug, the writers certainly had more than a bad piece of chicken when writing it.

I don’t know what Rocking Horse People are, but I know they eat Marshmallow Pie while The Girl with Kaleidoscope eyes is watching the Marmalade Skies and Plasticine Porters with Looking Glass Ties.

Right now, it looks like the AI revolution that has made the semiconductor market far more complex is about to enter its next, more chaotic phase, two and a half years after it began. Leaving Sgt. Pepper to enter the White insanity era.

While the cloud market has always been intricately connected to the semiconductor market, they can be viewed and analysed individually; that is about to end as the two markets merge into a higher level of complexity, but it might not be as insane as I thought when I first saw it.

Apart from the groundbreaking decision by Google and Amazon to enter the Semiconductor Device market by making their own AI Chips generally available, there were several other key developments.

The Agentic revolution is now visible, and the added inference workload will benefit Intel and AMD, as well as hyperscaler AI architectures purposely designed for this. All of the hyperscalers promoted that their customers have seen increased performance gains through agents. This drives a new business model from “Seat” to “Seat plus Consumption”, which sounds like selling rubber bands by the yard. A new level of trust is needed for that one. While the agentic workflow will impact the semiconductor industry, there is still limited tangible evidence to work with, so I will address that later.

There were also significant developments in physical AI, with more than 500K Google Waymo rides a week and 2M miles driven by Amazon Zoox. There were also the first signs of robotics activity.

Amazon pledged 20 more launches in 26 and 30 in 27, adding to the first 10 launches that have brought 250 Project Leo satellites into orbit.

A major strategic development in this space is Amazon’s planned acquisition of Globalstar. This acquisition provides Amazon with the scarce global spectrum and satellite expertise required to offer direct-to-device capabilities.

Amazon is leveraging this acquisition to establish a deep relationship with Apple. Amazon Leo will now natively power direct-to-device satellite services for Apple iPhones and Apple Watches.

Open space is now open for business.

The 4 major AI companies all decided to coordinate their earnings releases into a few hours. While this is a coincidence, it gave the markets a chance to respond rather than the normal flip-flopping between the calls.

The results were all strong, a few per cent above guidance as is customary, and the stock market was all over the place (as is also customary). This usually highlights that they were all operating in a highly controlled fashion, with little or no surprises.

In what is seasonally a down quarter, the 4 main hyperscalers grew revenue by over 20%, while profits increased compared to Q4.

Gross profit increased by 37% yoy while net profit increased by 62%. We might still be a bit away from the AI apocalypse.

Revenue growth from the Clouds was significantly stronger, as expected. The QoQ growth was 7%, while the annual growth was 33.5%. This is an acceleration over the long-term growth, which is 28% CAGR over the period shown.

Google's cloud revenue growth has been astonishing and demonstrates the benefits of having a full tech stack, from chips to native models. While Microsoft could claim the same, I have not heard of anyone using Co-Pilot unless they are forced by a corporate purchasing decision. While I have not tried it, I imagine an LLM with a lot of buttons and menus that ignores the user experience. I apologise for letting my bias briefly peek through.

The CapEx increased again, but was weaker than last quarters 21% growth. Quite astonishing to hear myself say that 9% QoQ is weak, but we have been spoiled over the last few years.

As most of the memory contracts are long-term agreements, it is probably still too early to see the effect on CapEx.

The CapEx guidance was strong across all major hyperscalers and was immediately reflected in the consensus of financial analysts.

While the future CapEx growth shows that the financial analysts know as much as my hairdresser about the 2027 forecast, they still have to run the numbers. They do, however, have a firm grip on the near-term forecast for 2026, which rose from 66% to 79% growth in just one quarter.

That is another $50B on top of the $250B forecasted last quarter. Soon, we'll be talking real money, as Warren Buffett would say.

Undoubtedly, we are seeing the impact of memory pricing on the CapEx forecast, and as memory contracts evolve from one- to multiyear agreements, the hyperscalers all have a pretty good idea of the near-term impact.

This is a problem emerging for the hyperscalers. One thing is that memory pricing is increasing dramatically, another is that it is rising due to high demand and low capacity. It is impossible to expand a data centre without allocating memory, and that should have been sorted out yesterday or the year before.

Up until now, the bottleneck has been GPUs, but rumours have it that they are not yet fully utilised, the power scarcity set in, and now it's memory. As I have said before, there are alternatives in power but not in memory.
