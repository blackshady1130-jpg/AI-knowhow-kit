## Abstract

Humans perceive far more in a scene than what is explicitly depicted: a single glance captures past causes and future trajectories; a quick peek determines if a vehicle can fit between two parked cars; a few seconds of video reveals who holds authority in a room; and a fleeting clip highlights subtle abstract patterns like unwritten rules or hidden labels. This capacity reflects a form of humanity’s sixth sense: an intuitive reasoning mechanism that recovers implicit information beyond raw sensory perception. Crucially, this rapid, zero-shot visual intuition underpins everyday navigation and social interaction, making it a vital capability for Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) deployed alongside people. Existing visual benchmarks, however, target either deliberate expert-level analysis in academic and mathematical domains or low-level perception, leaving the intuitive reasoning that people perform largely untested. To bridge this gap, we introduce Humanity’s Sixth Sense (HSS), a benchmark for intuitive visual reasoning. HSS spans diverse image and video inputs, organizes items under a struc- tured taxonomy, and pairs each with human-written prompts probing the implicit temporal, spatial, social, and abstract structure that people infer at a glance. Frontier MLLMs fall short of human performance: participants reach 93.1% accuracy, while the strongest model, GPT-6-astra, reaches only 53.6% even at maximum reasoning effort. Despite excelling in many complex tasks that require advanced

perception and knowledge, current models still struggle significantly on these visual tasks that are intuitive for humans. We further explore agentic setup that apply dynamic visual manipulation to HSS, which narrows but does not close the gap. HSS establishes intuitive visual reasoning as a measurable axis and directs attention to a capability that scaling on current benchmarks has so far left behind.

## 1. Introduction

A single glance at a visual scene reveals far more than what is explicitly rendered in pixels. Within a fraction of a second, we intuitively register who holds authority in a room, sense the subtle tension behind a polite smile, or notice a toddler edging toward the stairs, long before anything goes wrong. We call this capacity humanity’s sixth sense: a rapid, pre-verbal form of visual inference that transforms circumstantial evidence into actionable conclusions. For AI agents deployed in homes, vehicles, and workplaces, this skill is not a luxury, but a core necessity: an agent must perceive the world through a human lens so that its predictions and behaviors naturally align with the people around it. This capacity relies on visual intuition. In cognitive science, intuition is characterized as an experience-based process that yields spontaneous judgments without conscious derivation (Kotler et al., 2025), functioning as skilled pattern recognition in environments with learnable regularities (Kahneman & Klein, 2009). In human vision, this is remarkably concrete: people categorize natural scenes within roughly 150ms (Thorpe et al., 1996) while effortlessly extracting implied physical dynamics, human motives, and social structures (Spelke & Kinzler, 2007; Battaglia et al., 2013; Baker et al., 2017). We synthesize these insights to define an inference as intuitive when it is fast, automatic, grounded in everyday experience rather than step-by-step derivation, and accessible even when its underlying rationale is difficult to articulate. In text-only domains, benchmarks like SimpleBench (Philip & Hemang, 2024) have shown that frontier models lag significantly behind humans on commonsense spatio-temporal and social reasoning. In vision, the closest counterpart is BabyVision (Chen et al., 2026), which highlights model failures on basic visual tasks that young children solve effortlessly. However, while BabyVision tests whether models perceive what is explicitly present, HSS evaluates whether they infer what is implicit. Visual scenes carry vast unstated context such as revealing interpersonal relationships, latent intentions, physical affordances, and imminent consequences without ever stating them. Existing visual benchmarks fail to probe this dimension: they either treat images as prompts for specialized academic problem-solving (e.g., MMMU (Yue et al., 2024), HLE (Phan et al., 2025)) or isolate narrow perceptual capabilities like visual searching and object tracking (e.g., V* (Wu & Xie, 2024), Video-MME (Fu et al., 2024a), Video-MME-v2 (Fu et al., 2026)). Meanwhile, existing probes of physical and social intelligence (Zellers et al., 2019; Riochet et al., 2022; Kamath et al., 2023; Zadeh et al., 2019; Bear et al., 2021; Jin et al., 2024) remain fragmented across isolated domains, rely heavily on synthetic environments, or depend on multiple-choice formats that allow models to succeed through process-of-elimination heuristics rather than true visual inference. To address this gap, we introduce Humanity’s Sixth Sense (HSS), a benchmark specifically targeting the domain of intuitive visual reasoning that operates between raw perception and deliberate, step-by-step logic. Concretely, an HSS task requires interpreting visual evidence alongside contextual, physical, social, and commonsense knowledge to deduce unstated implications, latent states, and likely outcomes. For instance, viewers must infer an agent’s intended goal, recognize physical affordances, reconstruct prior events, or anticipate imminent consequences purely from visual cues and everyday experience. HSS comprises open-ended tasks spanning four core domains: temporal & causal dynamics, physical & spatial logic, social understanding, and abstract contextual inference (Figure 2), categorized into 11 subdomains that reflect human scene interpretation (Section 2.2). To avoid multiple-choice artifacts, models generate free-form responses evaluated against human-annotated atomic rubrics. Our experiments reveal a substantial capability gap (Figure 1). Human participants achieve 93.1% pass rate on HSS, whereas the strongest model evaluated, GPT-6-astra, attains only 53.6% even at maximum reasoning effort. Moreover, standard scaling strategies do not close this gap: increased test-time deliberation, higher reasoning effort, and agentic tool use each yield only modest improvements, and performance remains far from saturation. ## 2. The HSS Benchmark

### 2.1 Task Format

Each benchmark instance is a tuple (m, q, g, R): a visual media m (a static image or a video clip), a natural-language question q, a human-authored reference answer g, and a set of atomic rubrics R = {r1,..., rk}, each independently checkable against g and mandatory for an answer to count as correct. Given m and q, a model returns a free-form answer a, and a judge marks every criterion

ri ∈ R as met or unmet given q, a, g, and R; an instance counts as solved only when all k are met (Section C.4).

### 2.2 HSS Taxonomy Design

Human intuitive visual reasoning enables us to infer unobserved dynamics, physical constraints, mental states, and contextual rules directly from visual input. Our taxonomy builds on three core domains established in cognitive science, including core knowledge of objects and space (Spelke & Kinzler, 2007), intuitive physics (Battaglia et al., 2013), and mentalizing (Baker et al., 2017), and further introducing an additional domain for abstract regularities implied by a scene. To render this latent capacity measurable, we formalize it into four core domains encompassing eleven subdomains (Table 4):

1. Temporal & Causal Dynamics (Time): Reconstructs the unobserved timeline connecting visual states, evaluating inferences about past events (Retrodiction), underlying physical drivers (Mechanistic Causality), and future trajectories (Extrapolation).
2. Physical & Spatial Logic (Space): Reasons over unshown content (Hidden & Invisible), functional interactions (Affordance & Feasibility), novel perspectives (Spatial “Alien Viewpoint”), and physical accessibility under spatial constraints (Spatial Reachability).
3. Social Understanding (Mind): Formalizes the invisible mental and normative substrate of human interaction, capturing intent and belief attribution (Theory of Mind) as well as implicit social expectations (Social Role, Norm & Power Dynamics).
4. Abstract & Contextual Inference (Context): Derives non-perceptual implications that a scene implies but never explicitly depicts, assessing unseen state transitions (Change & Consequence) and structural regularities (Patterns & Pareidolia). Rather than treating visual intuition as an unanalyzable or monolithic capacity, the taxonomy described above provides a principled foundation that fulfills a dual purpose: first, as a generative blueprint that enforces rigorous, multi-faceted coverage during benchmark curation; and second, as a fine-grained diagnostic framework for model evaluation. By projecting performance across these eleven cognitive dimensions rather than relying solely on aggregate scores, the taxonomy disentangles distinct competency profiles and exposes the specific latent reasoning gaps of state-of-the-art multimodal models.

### 2.3 Task Design Principles

Because intuitive visual reasoning inherently risks subjective interpretation, task curation in HSS is strictly governed by three core design principles:

1. Exclusion of Pure Perception: Tasks must require latent inference beyond what is explicitly depicted in the visual media. We reject tasks that rely purely on fine-grained visual recognition, regardless of visual complexity. If a high-resolution crop of the target region makes the answer immediately obvious, the task evaluates visual acuity rather than intuitive reasoning and is discarded.
2. Exclusion of Specialized Expertise: Tasks must target intuitive reasoning accessible to any attentive adult. We strictly prohibit items requiring symbolic calculation, specialized academic knowledge, or niche professional training, ensuring the benchmark purely isolates commonsense visual intuition.
3. Strict Human Consensus and Unambiguity: Both questions and target answers must command unambiguous human consensus. Prompts are carefully structured to prevent subjective or divergent interpretations, and a task is admitted into the benchmark only if independent reviewers reach unanimous agreement on the ground truth.

### 2.4 Benchmark Construction Pipeline

Each task in HSS is authored by a trained human annotator through a three-step workflow: (1) selecting a source image or video clip from a curated media pool; (2) assigning the item to one of

our four core domains and eleven subdomains; and (3) composing a natural-language prompt q, a gold reference answer g, and a structured rubric R decomposing g into fine-grained, independently checkable criteria. Authored tasks then enter a rigorous quality-control pipeline consisting of three independent review rounds. At each stage, a reviewer renders one of three verdicts: Pass: Advances the task to the subsequent review stage. Edit: Returns the task to the authoring phase for revision with specific feedback, after which it must re-enter the review pipeline from the beginning. Drop: Permanently removes the item if the prompt is ambiguous, the visual media is uninformative, or the target inference fails to command unanimous human consensus. Only tasks that achieve unanimous approval across all three independent rounds are admitted into the benchmark, guaranteeing that every released rubric has been verified by at least three reviewers beyond its author. The review pipeline is highly lossy, and this attrition is the primary driver of dataset quality: out of 3,466 initially authored tasks, 1,164 survive the first review stage, 559 pass the second, and 522 pass the third, yielding an overall end-to-end acceptance rate of 15.1%. The most significant filter occurs at the first review gate, where approximately two-thirds of submitted tasks are filtered out, primarily for relying on pure visual perception or exhibiting subjective prompts and target answers. Appendix B (Figure 8) details the complete flowchart and attrition mechanics across each review stage. The final released benchmark comprises these 522 strictly vetted tasks across 4 domains and 11 subdomains (288 image-based and 234 video-based instances), carrying 723 fine-grained rubric criteria in total (Table 3).

## 3. Experiments

We evaluate 25 representative MLLMs on HSS. For models supporting native video inputs, we feed raw video files directly. Otherwise, we apply uniform frame sampling capped at 500 frames; for models with stricter input limits, we default to their maximum supported frame capacity. Static images are evaluated at native resolution, downscaling only when required by API or payload constraints. Model outputs are graded by Claude-Opus-5, serving as an automated LLM judge. Because the HSS data curation process retains only mandatory criteria and excludes non-essential ones, a response is marked as successful if and only if it satisfies all of the corresponding rubric criteria. Fur- ther details regarding the experimental setup, prompts, and evaluation protocol are in Appendix C.

### 3.2 Model Failure Analysis

To investigate why models fail, we categorize 8,573 failed first attempts from the main evaluation using an eleven-cause taxonomy grouped into three families. Perception: errors related to properties explicitly present in the visual signal (e.g., scale, geometry, facing direction, and spatial alignment). Extending beyond low-level object detection (e.g., recognizing a person or car), this involves evaluating visible visual evidence for intuitive reasoning. Latent Inference: errors related to information not directly rendered in pixels (e.g., unobserved past events, hidden quantities behind obstacles, or underlying motives), requiring the model to bridge the gap between what is seen and what remains unobserved or implied. Reasoning: errors related to logical deduction, where the model fails to synthesize correct explicit and latent visual facts to draw valid conclusions. As shown in Figure 3, failures stem predominantly from visual perception (53%) and latent inference (41%), whereas abstract reasoning accounts for only 5%. Overall, the two largest single failure modes are missing a decisive visual cue (21%) and misidentifying an object, person, or role (20%). Furthermore, failure patterns are highly systematic across architectures rather than model-specific: on tasks where five or more models fail, a median of 80% fail due to the exact same cause. Subdomains also exhibit distinct failure signatures: affordance and spatial reachability fail primarily on size and fit (62% and 56%), extrapolation on facing direction (42%), hidden & invisible on hidden quantities (44%), and mechanistic causality and theory of mind on missed cues (47% and 39%). Alien viewpoint is the only subdomain with a notable reasoning component, led by line-of-sight errors (23%). Extended failure analyses and case studies are provided in Appendices D and E.

### 3.3 Agentic Harnesses

To test whether active tool use aids performance on HSS, we evaluate base models inside vendorprovided agentic harnesses, including Claude Code and Codex across 388 tasks (a subset of HSS). As shown in Table 2, the agentic setup improves accuracy across all configurations, though performance gains and compute overhead vary widely. Claude Code provides substantial accuracy in- creases (+16.5 to +20.5 points) at high compute costs (median 57–72 steps; > 42k thinking tokens per task), whereas Codex yields more modest improvements (+4.9 to +7.5 points) with minimal overhead (14–18 steps). However, even the top-performing setup (Codex with GPT-6-astra) reaches only 59.3%, demonstrating that HSS remains far from saturated even when models are equipped with agentic capabilities. Re-labeling agentic failures using our error taxonomy reveals distinct patterns in where agent loops succeed and fail:

- Active re-inspection repairs perception, not spatial depth: Total failures decrease from 944 to 759 (Figure 4), driven by a 19–37% reduction in perception and latent errors (e.g., recovering missed cues or misidentified objects). However, 2D-to-3D projection errors persist, demonstrating that zooming into a 2D image cannot recover lost 3D spatial depth.
- Gains concentrate in evidence-rich subdomains: Subdomains where visual evidence is explicitly present in-frame benefit most, such as patterns & pareidolia (+30.6 points) and retrodiction (+18.9 points; Table 8). Tasks whose answer turns on depth or on an unobserved outcome gain least (e.g., spatial reachability at +4.8 points).
- Agentic failure dynamics: Agent loops introduce unique failure modes, occasionally overanalyzing inputs and talking themselves out of correct initial readings. Additionally, failed agent runs take significantly more steps than successful ones (median 81 vs. 67 steps). Appendix F reports all four harness/base pairs and provides four annotated trajectories; Appendix D details the complete failure analysis for agentic experiments.

### 3.4 Ablation Studies

Effects of Reasoning Effort. We evaluate the impact of reasoning effort on model performance across HSS subdomains (Figure 5). While increased effort generally improves overall performance, accuracy in certain subdomains degrades at higher reasoning levels. For example, in the retrodiction subdomain, GPT-6-astra exhibits a 14-point drop when scaling effort from high to xhigh. Consequently, peak accuracy is frequently achieved at intermediate effort levels rather than the max- imum setting. These findings suggest that explicit deliberation is not the primary driver of HSS performance, which aligns with human cognitive observations where similar competencies operate intuitively prior to deliberate reasoning. Extended ablation results are provided in Appendix G.

## 4. Related Work

Most current multi-modal benchmarks fall into two families. One wraps problem-solving with visual inputs, such as MMMU, MathVista, Humanity’s Last Exam (Yue et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024; Phan et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2025), where the image supplies explicit givens that could in principle be transcribed into text. The other targets perceptual acuity, clutter and content tracking, such as V ∗ MMBench, Video-MME, BLINK, Perception Test and MVBench (Wu & Xie, 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2024a;b; Pătrăucean et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024), a line that runs back to VQA v2 (Goyal et al., 2017). HSS is orthogonal to both: its answers are neither transcribable nor findable by looking harder, because what the task asks for was never in the frame. HSS is also related to a body of visual abduction benchmarks and to spatial, physical, social and abstract reasoning suites (Zellers et al., 2019; Hessel et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2024; Riochet et al., 2022; Xiao et al., 2021; Zadeh et al., 2019; Sap et al., 2019; Bear et al., 2021; Yi et al., 2020; Jin et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2019; Kamath et al., 2023). Each covers part of this territory, typically one competence at a time and often on synthetic scenes; HSS spans physical, spatial, social and abstract inference over natural media under a single admission standard (Appendix A). Our agentic condition follows work showing that active inspection, such as cropping, zooming, re-sampling, sketching can lift visual reasoning (Roberts et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2024), and our grading follows LLM-as-judge practice (Zheng et al., 2023) with an explicit audit of self-preference (Panickssery et al., 2024) (Section 3.4).

## 5. Limitations

We note three major limitations. Visual channel only. HSS evaluates what a model infers from what it can see. Video items are supplied without their audio track and without a transcript, so items whose resolution would depend on speech or off-screen sound are outside the benchmark’s scope by construction. Judge dependence. Scores are mediated by an LLM judge, which is known to carry biases including self-preference (Panickssery et al., 2024). We report cross-judge agreement and an oracle condition in which human reference answers are scored correct, which bound the effect.

Contamination. Media sourced from the public web may appear in training data. The questions are newly authored against that media rather than collected with it, which is what the benchmark tests, but prior exposure to an individual image or clip cannot be fully excluded.

## 6. Conclusion

Humans deduce far more from a visual scene than what is explicitly rendered in pixels: a capability measured directly by Humanity’s Sixth Sense (HSS). To benchmark visual intuition objectively, HSS operationalizes it through a structured taxonomy and a rigorous task construction pipeline. Across our benchmarks, frontier models exhibit a stark performance gap compared to the human baseline, a discrepancy that persists despite test-time compute scaling and integration into agentic harnesses. HSS highlights intuitive visual reasoning as a fundamental, measurable axis of multimodal intelligence where current scaling paradigms have so far yielded limited progress.

## Appendix A: Extended Related Work

This section expands Section 4 with the comparisons that place HSS against individual benchmarks rather than against families of them. Academic problem-solving in a visual wrapper. MMMU, MathVista, DynaMath and Humanity’s Last Exam (Yue et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024; Phan et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2025) test high-level symbolic or domain-specific reasoning over images: a diagram, a plot or a figure supplies givens, and the difficulty lives in the chain of reasoning applied to them. The distinguishing property is that a sufficiently complete verbal description of the image would preserve the task. HSS items do not have this property by measuring the intuitive visual reasoning that does not require deliberate step-by-step derivation. Perception-centred benchmarks. V ∗, MMBench, Video-MME, and Video-MME-v2 (Wu & Xie, 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2024a; 2026) test visual acuity, robustness to clutter, and tracking of content across duration. Here the answer is in the frame, and the question is whether the model can find it. HSS inverts the admission criterion (Section 2.3): an item is rejected precisely when a careful enough look would settle it. The two benchmark families are therefore complementary. Visual abduction and commonsense. VCR, VisualCOMET and Sherlock (Zellers et al., 2019; Park et al., 2020; Hessel et al., 2022) are the closest ancestors: each asks the model to go beyond the depicted scene to intentions, prior events or likely continuations. They concentrate on social and narrative context and grade plausible completions, often against multiple-choice alternatives. HSS covers physical, spatial, social and abstract inference under one taxonomy, and admits only items whose answer reviewers agree is forced rather than merely plausible, which is what makes open-ended rubric grading possible. Spatial, physical, and social reasoning. SpatialVLM, IntPhys, Physion and CLEVRER (Chen et al., 2024; Riochet et al., 2022; Bear et al., 2021; Yi et al., 2020) evaluate relation-naming, physical plausibility and collision prediction, largely over synthetic or simulated scenes; NExT-QA, Social- IQ, SocialIQa and MMToM-QA (Xiao et al., 2021; Zadeh et al., 2019; Sap et al., 2019; Jin et al., 2024) evaluate causal, social and mental-state understanding of observed events; VSI-Bench and What’s Up (Yang et al., 2025; Kamath et al., 2023) probe spatial memory and relational language; and RAVEN (Zhang et al., 2019) isolates abstract visual analogy. HSS extends both directions to natural media and to inferences about what is not observed: an alien viewpoint the camera never occupies, a rope whose tension is visible only in its sag, an event that the clip sets up but never shows. Tool-augmented inspection. ZeroBench and VisualToolBench (Roberts et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025) show that letting a model crop, zoom and re-sample improves visual reasoning, and motivate our agentic condition. Section 3.3 uses that lever diagnostically rather than as a method contribution: if HSS failures were failures of looking, active inspection should repair them, and the extent to which it does not (Figure 4) is evidence about what the benchmark measures. Automated grading and its validity. Open-ended grading at this scale requires an LLM judge (Zheng et al., 2023), which imports known failure modes, self-preference (Panickssery et al., 2024) foremost among them. We audit rather than assume: three judges from three vendors, agreement and κ per pair, and a self-grading contrast (Section 3.4, Appendix I).

### Appendix C.3 Judging

A single LLM judge grades all runs, following standard practice for free-form evaluation (Zheng et al., 2023). The judge sees the question, the reference answer, the rubric, and the candidate answer, and returns a per-criterion verdict with a one-sentence justification. It does not see the media by default: rubrics are written to be checkable against the reference answer, which keeps the judge from re-adjudicating perception. We report the effect of changing judge model in Section 3.4.

Grading protocol. The judge is instructed to: mark a criterion met only if the answer actually asserts what it requires; mark a criterion unmet if the answer does not state the required content; ignore extra correct detail; and treat the reference answer as ground truth on disagreement. It returns one verdict per criterion with a short justification. Unparseable output is re-requested a bounded number of times before the sample is recorded as a grading failure rather than a zero. The system prompt and output schema are in Appendix C.6.

This section expands the summary in Section 3.4. Pairwise agreement is 95.8– 97.2% at the criterion level and 96.2–97.5% at the item level, with Cohen’s κ between 0.914 and 0.946 on both units (Figure 6b); all three judges return the same verdict on 95.2% of items. The disagreement that remains is almost entirely a level shift rather than a reordering. GPT-5.6-terra is the strict judge, scoring every one of the five models on average 2.4 points below the mean of the other two, while Claude-Opus-5 and Gemini-3.6-flash sit within 0.3 points of each other on average. Spearman rank correlation between judges is exactly 1.0 for all three pairs, so no conclusion in this paper that rests on model ordering changes under a different grader. Self-preference is not detectable at this scale. LLM judges are known to favour their own family’s outputs (Panickssery et al., 2024), which matters here because our primary judge shares a vendor with multiple evaluated models. To evaluate potential vendor bias, we first calculated each judge’s overall leniency—defined as the difference between the score it gives a model and the average score given by the other two judges:

- Claude-Opus-5: +1.1 points,
- GPT-5.6-terra: -2.4 points,
- Gemini-3.6-flash: +1.3 points. Subtracting these baseline leniencies isolates same-vendor interaction effects (residuals):
- GPT-5.6-terra judging GPT-6-astra: -0.80 points,
- Gemini-3.6-flash judging Gemini-3.8-flash: +0.38 points,
- Claude-Opus-5 judging Claude-Fable-5: +1.09 points,
- Claude-Opus-5 judging itself: -0.44 points. All four residuals are small and two have negative signs (contradicting the expectation of selfpreference). We interpret this not as proof that vendor bias is entirely absent, but as finding no detectable evidence of it at the resolution HSS currently supports.
