# Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality

###### Abstract

Standard factuality evaluations of LLMs treat all errors alike, obscuring whether failures arise from missing knowledge (empty shelves) or from limited access to encoded facts (lost keys). We propose a behavioral framework that profiles factual knowledge at the level of facts rather than questions, characterizing each fact by whether it is encoded, and then by how accessible it is: cannot be recalled, can be directly recalled, or can only be recalled with inference-time computation (thinking). To support such profiling, we introduce WikiProfile, a new benchmark constructed via an automated pipeline with a prompted LLM grounded in web search. Across 4 million responses from 13 LLMs, we find that encoding is nearly saturated in frontier models on our benchmark, with GPT-5 and Gemini-3 encoding 95–98% of facts. However, recall remains a major bottleneck: many errors previously attributed to missing knowledge instead stem from failures to access it.
These failures are systematic and disproportionately affect long-tail facts and reverse questions.
Finally, we show that thinking improves recall and can recover a substantial fraction of failures, indicating that future gains may rely less on scaling and more on methods that improve how models utilize what they already encode.

### 1 Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) encode a vast amount of factual information in their parameters ( Joshi et al., 2017 ; Petroni et al., 2019 ; Kwiatkowski et al., 2019 ; Mallen et al., 2023 ) , yet factual errors remain a persistent challenge ( Wei et al., 2024a ; Haas et al., 2025 ; Ravichander et al., 2025 ) . While existing evaluations consider a response as either correct or incorrect, accuracy alone provides little insight into the source of the error. If an LLM incorrectly answers the question “Which famous band played their first gig at the Boardwalk club?” (the answer is Oasis), can we tell why? One possibility is that the LLM _does not encode_ this information in its parameters (e.g., due to limited data coverage or model capacity). Alternatively, a fact may be _encoded but not accessible_ under different conditions than how it was learned. One example is the reversal curse ( Berglund et al., 2024 ; Lin et al., 2024c ) , where an LLM can answer that Oasis played their first gig at the Boardwalk club, yet fails to answer who played their first gig there.

Encoding and recall failures are indistinguishable under accuracy metrics, yet they imply different limitations and solutions. Encoding failures call for pre-training interventions, such as scaling model size or data coverage. Recall failures suggest post-training interventions that often improve how models utilize what they already encode Zhou et al. (2023) ; Gekhman et al. (2024) ; Lin et al. (2024a) .

We therefore propose shifting the unit of analysis from individual questions to _facts_ . We introduce _knowledge profiling_ : a framework that categorizes facts into one of five profiles (see Figure [1](https://arxiv.org/html/.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") ), based on whether the fact is encoded, and by how accessible it is: cannot be recalled, can be directly recalled, or can be recalled only with inference-time computation (thinking). We use _thinking_ to refer to inference-time techniques that elicit intermediate computations before the final answer, including both chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting and dedicated thought tokens generated by thinking-optimized LLMs. We operationalize the definitions of encoding and recall behaviorally ( Holtzman and Tan, 2025 ) . We measure _encoding_ by priming the LLM to complete a factual proposition using a context that resembles how the fact was likely to be encountered during pre-training. We measure _recall_ ¹ ¹ 1 By analogy to human memory, we use _encoding_ to denote parametric representation, and _recall_ to accessing encoded facts. by asking questions about these encoded facts across different contexts, including phrasings or relational orderings. This enables the study of frontier LLMs despite the inaccessibility of their weights and training data.

To support knowledge profiling, we introduce WikiProfile , a new benchmark comprising 2,150 facts, each paired with 10 questions that probe encoding, recall, and recognition (multiple-choice). WikiProfile is constructed via a fully automated multi-step pipeline that employs a prompted LLM grounded in web search to generate, refine, and verify questions. Unlike other benchmarks that rely on synthetic or schema-restricted knowledge-base triplets ( Luo et al., 2023 ; Kumar et al., 2024 ; Yuan et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2025b ) , our facts are extracted from natural documents. This enables evaluation in more realistic settings, where facts are expressed indirectly, and may involve complex, context-dependent relations ( Wang et al., 2025b ) .

Using WikiProfile , we evaluate 13 LLMs, resulting in more than 4 million graded answers. We find that scaling primarily improves encoding, while recall remains a major bottleneck. For frontier LLMs, including Gemini-3-Pro and GPT-5, encoding is nearly saturated, with 95–98% of facts encoded. Yet the same models still cannot recall 25–33% of facts without thinking. In addition, we show that scaling model size alone increases what the model encodes but does not necessarily improve its recall.

Why do LLMs struggle to access knowledge they have encoded? Our findings suggest that recall is tightly coupled to the conditions under which facts were learned, degrading when queries diverge from training-time patterns. We manifest this through two cases: fact popularity and the reversal curse.
While the encoding gap between popular and long-tail facts for frontier LLMs is small (a few percentage points), the recall gap is substantially larger (often exceeding 25%). Reverse questions reveal a surprising pattern: LLMs struggle to answer reverse questions compared to direct ones, despite showing no disadvantage in recognizing the correct answer under multiple-choice evaluation.

Together, our results refine prior accounts of long-tail errors and the reversal curse ( Kandpal et al., 2023 ; Berglund et al., 2024 ; Mallen et al., 2023 ; He et al., 2025 ) . Whereas these failures are often interpreted as limitations of memorization or bidirectional encoding, our results suggest a different picture: rare facts are often encoded but inaccessible, and reverse facts can be recognized even when they cannot be generated. This reframes both phenomena as recall failures rather than _‘missing knowledge’_ . Our results also complement prior work on _‘hidden knowledge’_ ( Gekhman et al., 2025 ; Orgad et al., 2025 ) : what appears to be knowledge accessible only through model internals may reflect encoded facts whose recall is context-dependent, such that the right prompt or context can elicit ( Jiang et al., 2020 ) .

Given that encoding in frontier LLMs is nearing saturation while substantial headroom remains for recall, future improvements are likely to come from better utilization of existing knowledge. One mechanism that already demonstrates this is thinking: our results show that thinking recovers 40–65% of encoded-but-not-directly-known facts. The gains are most pronounced in challenging settings, such as long-tail facts and reverse questions. This parallels the “tip-of-the-tongue” phenomenon in human memory ( Brown and McNeill, 1966 ; Schwartz, 2002 ) , in which additional effort can surface knowledge that is present but momentarily inaccessible. Post-training and inference-time methods may thus play a central role in factuality by improving how models utilize what they already encode.

In summary, our contributions: (1) We propose a behavioral framework that characterizes factual knowledge in LLMs by separating encoding from recall via knowledge profiles; (2) We introduce WikiProfile , a benchmark for knowledge profiling, along with a fully automated pipeline for constructing such benchmarks; and (3) Through evaluation of 13 LLMs on Wikipedia-derived facts, we show that the bottleneck for factuality is recall, not encoding, and that thinking helps recover otherwise inaccessible knowledge.

### 2 Knowledge Profiling

We focus on _factual knowledge_ as the ability to answer short-form questions correctly without external tools. Our aim is to provide a systematic, model-agnostic characterization of factual behavior that applies to both closed- and open-weight LLMs. To this end, we introduce two complementary operational notions, defined purely in terms of observable behavior: _encoding_ and _knowledge_ . Together, they allow us to determine whether errors arise from encoding or recall, thereby informing different paths to improvement.

#### 2\.1 Operationalizing Encoding and Knowledge

Facts and Questions: We define a _fact_ as a proposition ² ² 2 Although facts are often described as subject-relation-object triplets, in our data they may involve multiple or complex relations and are therefore treated as propositions . involving an ordered pair of entities: a subject and an object. The roles of subject and object are determined by the source text from which the fact was extracted (e.g., a Wikipedia document): the subject is the entity that appears first in the text, and the object appears subsequently. A question whose answer is the object is termed a _direct question_ , while a question whose answer is the subject is termed a _reverse question_ . Examples are shown in Figure [2](https://arxiv.org/html/.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2.1 Operationalizing Encoding and Knowledge ‣ 2 Knowledge Profiling ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") .

For each question $q$ , we generate $n$ responses from the LLM and use a prompted LLM grader (autorater) to compare each response to the gold answer and label it as correct or incorrect. ³ ³ 3 The grader may assign ‘partially’ or ‘other’ labels when a response cannot be reliably evaluated. See § [4](https://arxiv.org/html/ "4 Experimental Setup ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") . Let $c_{q}$ and $i_{q}$ denote the number of responses to question $q$ graded as correct and incorrect. We define the _question grade_ as the accuracy over gradable responses:

|$g(q)=\frac{c_{q}}{c_{q}+i_{q}}.$ |
| --- |

Encoding: The notion of _encoding_ has been defined in multiple ways in prior work, typically referring to information stored in the model’s internal parametric representations. Existing approaches to measuring encoding often rely on access to internal states, a requirement that does not align with our focus on evaluating frontier LLMs. Moreover, the extent to which these methods reliably capture whether a model truly encodes a fact remains an open question ( Hase et al., 2023 ; Ma et al., 2023 ; Huang et al., 2024 ; Wei et al., 2024b ; Chen et al., 2025 ; Haller et al., 2025 ) .

We therefore adopt an _encoding-via-memorization_ perspective: an LLM encodes a fact if it can correctly reproduce that fact in a pre-training-like context. Intuitively, successful reproduction under training-like conditions provides sufficient evidence that the fact is encoded (though it may not be known). Since encoding, under this definition, is assessed behaviorally, we operationalize it using tasks that strongly prime the model with the context in which the fact $f$ originally appeared. We denote the set of such encoding tasks by $\mathcal{E}_{f}$ . We say an LLM _encodes_ the fact $f$ if:

|$\exists q\in\mathcal{E}_{f},\quad g(q)>\tau,$ |
| --- |

where $\tau$ is a predefined threshold. We set $\tau=0.5$ in all experiments as a natural majority threshold. We show in Appendix [D.2](https://arxiv.org/html/.SS2 "D.2 Threshold Selection ‣ Appendix D Methodological and Design Choices ‣ Appendix ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") that our findings are robust to this choice.

Refer to caption Figure 2 : _Top:_ We extract facts from Wikipedia, a predominant source of pre-training data. _Left:_ We measure encoding by prompting the LLM to reproduce facts within their original context, testing whether they are stored in the model’s parameters. _Right:_ We measure knowledge by asking questions across varied phrasings and relational directions, with and without thinking.

In our setup, $\mathcal{E}_{f}$ consists of two tasks.
The first task is _proposition completion_ (see Figure [2](https://arxiv.org/html/.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2.1 Operationalizing Encoding and Knowledge ‣ 2 Knowledge Profiling ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") ), in which the model is given the _left context_ (the entire source text truncated immediately before the object entity), and is asked to complete the factual statement. This task directly mimics the pre-training objective for which the LLM was optimized. Notably, the answer (the object entity) does not appear in the left context.

The second task, which we refer to as _contextual questioning_ , uses the same left context but replaces the final sentence with a direct question about the fact. We include both tasks because completion-based evaluation can be ambiguous for post-trained LLMs, which are optimized to answer questions rather than continue pre-training documents. LLMs may continue a sentence in multiple plausible ways that do not explicitly state the target fact. The contextual questioning task mitigates this ambiguity by explicitly eliciting the target fact in question form while preserving the original source context, which primes the model.

We deliberately exclude _thinking_ from the encoding evaluation. Correct reproduction can arise not only from direct encoding but also from inference, including multi-hop reasoning or educated guessing based on other encoded facts. To avoid conflating encoding with inference, encoding is always measured without thinking. Knowledge, in contrast, is evaluated both with and without thinking.

Knowledge: We say that an LLM knows a fact if it can correctly answer questions about it across a range of semantically equivalent contexts. This definition captures the intuition that factual knowledge should be robust to superficial changes, such as in phrasing and to alternative relational directions ( Elazar et al., 2021 ; Meng et al., 2022 ; Zucchet et al., 2025 ) . Let $\mathcal{Q}_{f}$ denote a set of questions associated with a fact $f$ . We say an LLM _knows_ a fact $f$ if:

|$\forall q\in\mathcal{Q}_{f},\quad g(q)>\tau,$ |
| --- |

The contrast with encoding is intentional: encoding uses existential quantification ( $\exists$ ) because reproducing a fact in any priming context suffices as evidence of storage, whereas knowledge uses universal quantification ( $\forall$ ) because robust recall should not depend on phrasing or query direction. In our setup, $\mathcal{Q}_{f}$ consists of four questions: two direct and two reverse, each in a different phrasing. This tests invariance to both surface form and relational direction.

#### 2\.2 Knowledge Profiles

Given our operational definitions, each fact can be characterized along three dimensions: whether it is _encoded_ , whether it is _known without thinking_ , and whether it is _known only with thinking_ . The interaction of these dimensions yields qualitatively distinct _knowledge profiles_ . In particular, we identify five profiles, illustrated in Figure [1](https://arxiv.org/html/.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") .

Encoding Failure: The fact is neither encoded nor known, with and without thinking. A high rate of encoding failures indicates limitations in model capacity or pre-training data coverage, suggesting interventions such as increasing model size or expanding the training set.

Recall Failure: The fact is encoded but not known, even with thinking. This indicates that the fact is stored but inaccessible at inference time, and calls for improvements beyond pre-training, such as in post-training.

Direct Recall: The fact is encoded and known without thinking. This straightforward recall reflects highly accessible knowledge, which is desirable in many applications (e.g., when integrating facts into long-form responses).

Recall with Thinking: The fact is encoded but known only with thinking. This indicates that the fact is not readily accessible and requires additional computation to be recalled. This profile can be viewed as a recovery mechanism for otherwise inaccessible facts.

Inference without Encoding: The fact is not encoded but is known when thinking is enabled. In this case, correctness may arise from inference over other encoded facts, such as multi-hop reasoning or educated guessing. It is also possible that the fact is encoded but missed by our encoding tasks. This profile is less reliable; relying on it may promote hallucinations, and it occurs less frequently in practice.

Finally, we do not define a profile for facts that are not encoded but known without thinking. While such cases are possible, they occur in fewer than 0.5% of instances across all evaluated models and are likely attributable to sampling noise or grader error. We therefore exclude this profile.

### 3 The WikiProfile Benchmark

To operationalize the knowledge profiles, we need a benchmark that supports measuring both encoding and knowledge. We hence introduce WikiProfile , a benchmark for evaluating and profiling factual knowledge in LLMs. Since profiling involves measuring encoding, we extract facts from Wikipedia, a trusted reference that pre-training pipelines are likely to prioritize, making it both a strong candidate for encoded knowledge and a reliable ground truth. WikiProfile consists of 2150 facts, each with ten corresponding questions. These include two questions for measuring encoding (proposition-completion and contextual questions); four questions for measuring knowledge (direct and reverse questions, each instantiated with two distinct phrasings); and four multiple-choice variants for measuring fact verification capabilities.
Examples of tasks are in Table [1](https://arxiv.org/html/.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Controlled Properties ‣ A.1 Tasks and Properties ‣ Appendix A WikiProfile – Full Details ‣ Appendix ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") .

All questions in WikiProfile have a single, unambiguous gold answer, enabling automatic validation with an LLM grader (see § [4](https://arxiv.org/html/ "4 Experimental Setup ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") ). WikiProfile is constructed through a fully automated pipeline (Gemini-2.5-Pro with thinking; Comanici et al. 2025 ) that may be applicable to other corpora for domain-specific knowledge profiling.

Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") .
All prompts in the pipeline were carefully developed through manual optimization and error analysis on a held-out subset of tens of documents, from which the examples used in the prompts are drawn.

Fact Extraction: To extract facts, we sample 10,000 Wikipedia pages, record their visit counts, and categorize them into nine topics (see Figures [9](https://arxiv.org/html/.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ Controlled Properties ‣ A.1 Tasks and Properties ‣ Appendix A WikiProfile – Full Details ‣ Appendix ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") and [10](https://arxiv.org/html/.F10 "Figure 10 ‣ Filtering ‣ A.3 Question Generation Process ‣ Appendix A WikiProfile – Full Details ‣ Appendix ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") for distributions). Next, we perform NER to identify entities and their types (e.g., DATE, LOCATION, PERSON; see Figure [11](https://arxiv.org/html/.F11 "Figure 11 ‣ Reverse Questions ‣ A.3 Question Generation Process ‣ Appendix A WikiProfile – Full Details ‣ Appendix ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") ). For each document, we select up to 3 candidate object entities (not from the first sentences), each of which defines a distinct fact. These candidates are selected based on strict criteria: an entity is suitable only if it represents a non-trivial, non-guessable, and time-independent (i.e., not subject to change) completion of its left context. Furthermore, the left context must constrain the completion to a single, unambiguous answer. After a final verification step, we downsample to 5,000 candidates, balancing categories and entity types. The median is 100 words, the mean is 116 words ( $\pm$ 56 std).

Question Generation: We generate direct and reverse questions through a three-step process of generation, refinement, and filtering, ensuring that each question is unambiguous, specific, minimal, and has a unique answer. Direct questions are generated first, and reverse questions are then derived by swapping subject and object roles. Because our goal is to test encoding, we initially generate high-verbatim questions that closely match the source text; we then create natural rephrasings of each to test robustness to phrasing variation. Contextual questions are constructed by appending the direct question to its left context, providing maximal priming. For each question, we also generate a multiple-choice variant with three plausible distractors matched by entity type and thematic similarity. All questions undergo filtering grounded in Google Search. We prompt an LLM with each question and discard cases where multiple answers are returned or clarification is needed. This filtering is strict: if any question is rejected, the entire fact is discarded, leaving us with approximately 2,200 facts.

Finally, we manually validated facts and their associated questions, discarding fewer than 2% of facts and yielding a final set of 2,150 facts. ### 4 Experimental Setup

LLMs: We evaluate 13 LLMs from five families, covering commercial and open-weight models: Gemini-3 and Gemini-2.5 (Pro and Flash variants) ( Comanici et al., 2025 ) ; GPT-5.2, GPT-5, and GPT-5-mini ( OpenAI, 2025b ) ; GPT-4.1 and GPT-4.1-mini ( OpenAI, 2025a ) ; and Gemma3 (1B, 4B, 12B, 27B) ( Kamath et al., 2025 ) . Each model is evaluated with and without thinking. Gemini-2.5, Gemini-3, and GPT-5 are thinking-optimized; we use default settings when thinking is enabled and set effort/budget to zero to disable it. For GPT-4.1 and Gemma3, we use CoT prompting.

Response Generation: For each example (an incomplete text or a question), we generate eight responses per LLM with a temperature of 1. In Appendix [D.4](https://arxiv.org/html/.SS4 "D.4 Multiple Response Generation ‣ Appendix D Methodological and Design Choices ‣ Appendix ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") , we justify the choice of eight responses. We employ three prompts, one for each task format (completion, closed-book QA, and multiple-choice QA). Each prompt instructs the LLM to produce concise, answer-only responses (see Box [E.3](https://arxiv.org/html/.SS3 "E.3 Response Instructions and Grader Prompts ‣ Appendix E Prompts ‣ Appendix ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? In total, we evaluate $\approx$ 4\.5 million responses (13 $\times$ 2 LLMs $\times$ 2150 facts $\times$ 10 tasks $\times$ 8 responses).

### 5 Results

Our results suggest that factual errors arise not from _missing knowledge_ (empty shelves), but from _inaccessible knowledge_ (lost keys). We first characterize this recall bottleneck by examining knowledge profiles across all 13 evaluated models (§ [5\.1](https://arxiv.org/html/.SS1 "5.1 The Recall Bottleneck ‣ 5 Results ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") ). We then ask why recall fails, identifying two systematic patterns, fact popularity and question directionality, where encoded knowledge remains difficult to access (§ [5\.2](https://arxiv.org/html/.SS2 "5.2 Why Recall Fails ‣ 5 Results ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") ). Finally, we examine thinking as a mechanism that compensates for these weaknesses, recovering facts that direct generation cannot surface (§ [5\.3](https://arxiv.org/html/.SS3 "5.3 Thinking as a Recovery Mechanism ‣ 5 Results ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") ). Due to the analysis scale, we occasionally report results for frontier LLMs only; complete results and details are provided in Appendix [C](https://arxiv.org/html/ "Appendix C Additional Results ‣ Appendix ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") .

#### 5\.1 The Recall Bottleneck

We begin by examining knowledge failures, namely, facts that are not known. These correspond to two profiles: _Encoding Failures_ and _Recall Failures_ . Figure [4](https://arxiv.org/html/.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4 Experimental Setup ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") shows the distribution of all five knowledge profiles across 13 LLMs.

Encoding is saturated; recall is not. For frontier LLMs such as Gemini-3-Pro and GPT-5, factual encoding is near saturation, with 95-98% of facts encoded. Yet these models fail to directly recall 26–34% of the facts, or 11–12% even with thinking. Accordingly, recall failures account for more than 70% of GPT-5.2’s errors and a larger share in stronger models, suggesting recall is indeed a bottleneck.

Scaling improves encoding more than recall. The Gemma3 model family illustrates this clearly. Increasing model capacity and size from 1B to 27B parameters reduces encoding failures from 85% to 23%. However, as model scale increases, a growing fraction of errors shifts from encoding failures to recall failures, peaking at 33% (with thinking) and 40% (without). In this family, scaling increases what the model stores; it does not necessarily improve what it can access at inference time.

#### 5\.2 Why Recall Fails

Having established that recall is the bottleneck, we now ask: what makes it difficult? To answer this, we next focus on encoded facts. We show that recall remains tied to the conditions under which facts were acquired. During pre-training, facts are encountered in specific contexts, phrasings, and orderings. When queries diverge from the training-time patterns, recall becomes harder. We examine two manifestations of this: fact popularity and question directionality.

Rare facts are encoded but hard to recall. We compare facts from low-popularity Wikipedia pages (bottom 20% by page views, used as a proxy for rare facts) with those from high-popularity pages (top 20%). Figure [5](https://arxiv.org/html/.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 5.2 Why Recall Fails ‣ 5 Results ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") presents, for the two tiers, the percentage of encoded facts and, among encoded facts, the percentage that are directly recalled. Conditioning on encoded facts isolates recall from encoding, allowing us to assess whether rare facts are harder to recall even when they are encoded. First, we find that rare facts are encoded at high rates approaching those of popular facts. Second, while the _encoding gap_ between popular and rare facts is modest (e.g., for Gemini-3-Pro, 99.5% vs. 94.5%, $\Delta\!=\!5$ ), the _recall gap_ is far larger (84.6% vs. 63.3%, $\Delta\!=\!21.3$ ); this pattern holds for all frontier LLMs. For smaller models like Gemma3-4B, encoding gaps are much larger; this is consistent with our broader finding that scaling primarily improves encoding.

Reframing the long-tail problem. Prior work established that LLMs struggle with long-tail (rare) facts, typically interpreted as a capacity limitation ( Kandpal et al., 2023 ; Mallen et al., 2023 ; Sun et al., 2024 ) . Our results suggest a complementary picture: rare facts _are_ encoded; however, recalling them is the challenge. Currently, the bottleneck has shifted from knowledge acquisition to utilization. Consequently, improving factuality in frontier LLMs will likely require interventions that enhance recall, not only scale.

#### 5\.3 Thinking as a Recovery Mechanism

We now turn to the question of what enables the recovery of otherwise inaccessible knowledge. To this end, we examine the potential of _thinking_ to fill this role. Thinking-optimized LLMs such as Gemini-3, Gemini-2.5, and GPT-5 models allocate additional computation to thinking by default; for non-thinking models (Gemma3 and GPT-4.1), we elicit similar behavior through CoT prompting.

Thinking targets recall weaknesses. Figure [7](https://arxiv.org/html/.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ 5.2 Why Recall Fails ‣ 5 Results ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") presents the improvement from thinking on encoded facts (i.e., recall gains), broken down by popularity and directionality. Thinking yields larger gains precisely where direct recall is weakest. For Gemini-3-Pro, thinking improves the recall of rare facts by 20.1 points, versus 11.3 for popular facts; for reverse questions, GPT-5 gains 19 points, versus 12 for direct questions. Consequently, thinking narrows both the popularity gap (from $\Delta\!=\!21.4$ to $12.5$ ) and the directionality gap (from $\Delta\!=\!9$ to $2$ ).

Why does thinking help? Thinking gains are remarkable because our facts are single-hop; they require no complex multi-step reasoning. Why should thinking help recall a simple fact such as _“Where did Oasis play their first show?”_ We consider three candidate mechanisms (with additional details in Appendix [B.1](https://arxiv.org/html/.SS1 "B.1 Three Possible Mechanisms ‣ Appendix B Thinking and Recall ‣ Appendix ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") ) through which thinking improves performance:
(i) _Response diversity_ : thinking increases output variance, improving the chance that at least one sample is correct (a sampling effect);
(ii) _Inference_ : thinking enables multi-step derivation from other encoded facts;
(iii) _Recall facilitation_ : thinking helps the model locate and retrieve a specific encoded fact that direct generation fails to access.

Figure 8 : Recovering Depends on Encoding: We report the percentage of not-known facts that become known with thinking, conditioned on whether the fact is encoded (red) or not (yellow). Thinking recovers 40–65% of encoded facts in thinking-optimized LLMs, but only 5–15% of non-encoded facts. This dependence on encoding is consistent with the recall facilitation mechanism.

Our analysis in Appendix [B.1](https://arxiv.org/html/.SS1 "B.1 Three Possible Mechanisms ‣ Appendix B Thinking and Recall ‣ Appendix ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") rules out response diversity as the main mechanism that improves factuality for thinking-optimized LLMs. When thinking is enabled, models become more consistent in their correctness, producing a higher fraction of correct samples for the same question. This pattern is incompatible with increased output variance. We therefore believe that thinking primarily facilitates recall of encoded knowledge.
Figure [8](https://arxiv.org/html/.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ 5.3 Thinking as a Recovery Mechanism ‣ 5 Results ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") provides evidence that supports this: thinking recovers 40–65% of encoded but not directly known facts in thinking-optimized LLMs, but only 5–20% of non-encoded facts. This dependence on encoding status is expected under recall facilitation. It is also partially expected under inference, since non-encoded facts may lack the related encoded premises that inference requires. Nonetheless, our facts are single-hop, and complex deductive reasoning is unnecessary to derive the correct answer. For single-hop facts, recall facilitation is the more parsimonious explanation: the model already stores the answer and need not derive it from other knowledge. We therefore conclude that thinking primarily helps models access knowledge that they have already encoded. Gekhman et al. (2026) concurrently show that thinking expands parametric recall through computational buffering and priming.

The cost of thinking. While thinking improves recall, it is computationally expensive. Across frontier models, 10–20% of facts are accessible _only_ via thinking.
Determining exactly when to invoke thinking is
fundamentally a metacognitive problem, as it requires the model to recognize when direct recall is insufficient before triggering more intensive computation Yona et al. (2026) . Improving direct recall could help side-step this issue until models develop improved metacognitive abilities.

#### 5\.4 Connections to Human Cognition

The role of thinking in supporting recall resonates with well-studied phenomena in human memory. The _tip-of-the-tongue_ phenomenon describes states in which a person is confident they know something but cannot immediately produce it ( Brown and McNeill, 1966 ; Schwartz, 2002 ) . Such states are often resolved through deliberate effort: thinking of related concepts or mentally retracing the context in which the information was encountered. The parallel to our findings is suggestive: encoded-but-not-known facts in LLMs may represent a functional analogue, in which the information is stored but the query is insufficient to trigger recall. Thinking provides the additional computation that, like deliberate effort in humans, bridges the gap.

Separately, our verification results (in multiple-choice settings, § [5\.2](https://arxiv.org/html/.SS2 "5.2 Why Recall Fails ‣ 5 Results ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") ) echo the _feeling-of-knowing_ phenomenon: people often predict they will recognize an answer even when they cannot recall it ( Hart, 1965 ; Nelson and Narens, 1990 ) . The dissociation, in which LLMs can verify (recognize) answers to reverse questions but cannot generate (recall) them, mirrors this asymmetry in human memory. While we do not claim that LLMs implement human-like cognitive processes, the functional parallels suggest that the gap between encoding and recall may be a general property of systems in which the conditions for storing information diverge from the conditions for recalling it.

### 6 Related Work

Encoding and Memorization: Our behavioral definition of encoding connects to work on memorization in LLMs ( Carlini et al., 2023 ; Huang et al., 2024 ; Lu et al., 2024 ; Menta et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2025a ) . In particular, our encoding criterion aligns with the probabilistic framework of Hayes et al. (2025) . Memorization work typically focuses on privacy or data leakage concerns, whereas we use encoding-via-memorization for knowledge profiling.

Latent Knowledge: A separate line of work uses internal representations to argue that LLMs possess “latent knowledge” or “hidden knowledge”: facts they encode but fail to express in generation ( Kadavath et al., 2022 ; Burns et al., 2023 ; Gottesman and Geva, 2024 ; Gekhman et al., 2025 ; Orgad et al., 2025 ; Simhi et al., 2025 ) .
One possible reason is that the post-training phase may introduce new knowledge that undermines the use of the knowledge acquired during pre-training ( Gekhman et al., 2024 ; Kaplan et al., 2026 ) . Our results offer a complementary behavioral perspective: what appears to be latent knowledge may reflect context-dependent recall. Facts surface when queries match training conditions (as in our encoding tasks) but become harder to access when they diverge (as in our knowledge tasks).

The Reversal Curse: Berglund et al. (2024) documented that LLMs trained on “A is B” often fail to answer “What is B?”.
Subsequent work has attributed this to the autoregressive objective ( Guo et al., 2024 ; Kitouni et al., 2024 ; Lv et al., 2024 ) , training dynamics ( Zhu et al., 2024 ) , or data asymmetries ( Golovneva et al., 2024 ; He et al., 2025 ) , with proposed solutions focusing on pre-training modifications ( Golovneva et al., 2024 ; Yu et al., 2025 ) or architectural changes ( Wang and Sun, 2025 ) .
By comparing verification to generation, we show that LLMs can recognize reverse answers they cannot produce, suggesting the curse reflects a _recall asymmetry_ rather than a failure to learn bidirectional associations. Thinking can mitigate the curse, providing a path to improvement that prior work has not explored.

Thinking and Factuality: The effect of CoT prompting ( Wei et al., 2022 ; Wang et al., 2024b ) and reasoning-optimized models ( Marjanovic et al., 2025 ) on factuality remains contested. Some studies report improved accuracy via increased diversity ( Wang et al., 2023 ; Yang et al., 2025 ) , while others find higher hallucination rates ( Wang et al., 2024a ; Yao et al., 2025 ) or attribute gains to multi-hop reasoning ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) . Effects on consistency are similarly mixed, with some studies reporting improvements ( Kim et al., 2025 ; Wu et al., 2025a ) and others reporting degradation ( Cheng et al., 2025a ; Cheng et al., 2025b ) . A contemporaneous work by Gekhman et al. (2026) also demonstrated that thinking can expand the boundary of parametric knowledge recall, identifying two key mechanisms underlying this effect: a computational buffer and factual priming. Our work offers a unifying perspective: thinking functions as a recall mechanism, not just a reasoning mechanism. Its effect is most pronounced under challenging conditions, such as rare facts and reverse questions.

### 7 Discussion

In this work, we propose a framework for knowledge profiling and apply it to profile 13 LLMs across a benchmark of naturally occurring facts from Wikipedia. Our results point to recall, not encoding, as the primary bottleneck for factuality in frontier LLMs. Across the models we evaluated, encoding is approaching saturation; the strongest LLMs encode over 95% of facts in our benchmark. The challenge lies in accessing what has been learned. Recall failures are not random; they concentrate on rare facts and reverse questions, conditions where queries diverge from training-time contexts. This pattern suggests that LLMs store facts in ways that remain tied to their acquisition, making recall sensitive to surface-level query characteristics.

This shift from encoding to recall has implications beyond short-form factuality. Distinguishing between encoding and recall failures provides a framework that could extend to long-form generation, multi-step reasoning, and other domains where accessing learned knowledge is critical. In the age of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and tool-using agents, it is tempting to view parametric knowledge as secondary, something RAG can compensate for. But parametric knowledge is essential for fluency, speed, and integration across contexts. Improving how models access what they already encode is, therefore, a meaningful target.

The gap between encoding and recall might be bridged through intervention during _pre-training_ , for example, by augmenting data with self-generated question-answer pairs Lin et al. (2025) , or during _post-training_ , as recent studies suggest that alignment teaches models how to better utilize knowledge acquired during pre-training Zhou et al. (2023) ; Gekhman et al. (2024) ; Lin et al. (2024a) . Our findings also suggest that _inference-time_ techniques, such as thinking, can be effective in aiding parametric recall.

Our study has several limitations worth noting. WikiProfile is scoped to Wikipedia, an encyclopedic domain of relatively prominent facts, and our findings may not generalize to other corpora. Our operational definition of knowledge is likewise bounded: we measure recall along two axes, directionality and phrasing, for single-hop facts, and do not claim to exhaustively characterize all forms in which a fact can be queried. Finally, fully profiling a frontier model on WikiProfile costs $\pm$ $500, though this can be reduced substantially by omitting multiple-choice questions or using fewer samples per question.

### Impact Statement

This paper presents work aimed at understanding factual errors in LLMs. Our findings could inform deployment decisions by clarifying when models are likely to fail (e.g., with rare facts or reverse questions) and when inference-time computation may be beneficial. We do not foresee negative societal consequences specific to this work.

### Appendix A WikiProfile – Full Details

In this section, we describe WikiProfile and its construction in detail. WikiProfile comprises ten tasks defined for each fact extracted from a Wikipedia source text; their properties and examples are summarized in Table [1](https://arxiv.org/html/.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Controlled Properties ‣ A.1 Tasks and Properties ‣ Appendix A WikiProfile – Full Details ‣ Appendix ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") . The tasks span three formats: text completion, closed-book question answering, and multiple-choice question answering. Two tasks measure encoding (proposition completion and a contextual question), and four tasks measure knowledge (direct and reverse questions, each with two phrasings). Each knowledge task additionally has a multiple-choice variant.

WikiProfile is constructed using a fully automated pipeline powered by a prompted LLM, Gemini-2.5-Pro with thinking enabled ( Comanici et al., 2025 ) . Our goal is to provide a framework that can be readily applied to other corpora, including domain-specific ones, enabling researchers to identify knowledge profiles and characterize LLM behavior. Prompts were developed through manual optimization on a small subset of approximately 100 documents and facts, which is excluded from the final dataset. This process involved iterative error analysis and refinement.

#### A.1 Tasks and Properties

We begin by outlining the properties that guided the design of WikiProfile.

##### Required Properties

All questions must satisfy the following properties to ensure methodological rigor and enable comparison against a single verifiable gold answer:

* nosep, leftmargin=\*

  Answerable: A correct answer exists.
* nosep, leftmargin=\*

  Unambiguous: The question is self-contained and precisely phrased.
* nosep, leftmargin=\*

  Specific: The expected answer type and level of detail are clearly specified.
* nosep, leftmargin=\*

  Single-answer: Exactly one answer is correct.
* nosep, leftmargin=\*

  Not time-sensitive: The answer is stable over time.

##### Controlled Properties

These properties define systematic variations across tasks. By manipulating them, we examine factors that affect factual knowledge.

* nosep, leftmargin=\*

  Format: Completion, closed-book, or multiple-choice.
* nosep, leftmargin=\*

  Context: The amount of auxiliary information provided, ranging from full source context (used to measure encoding) to minimal context required for disambiguation.
* nosep, leftmargin=\*

  Verbatim: The degree of linguistic overlap with the source text, spanning exact phrasing (for completion tasks), high-verbatim questions that reuse source phrases and terms, and low-verbatim, natural user-like queries.
* nosep, leftmargin=\*

  Direction: The relational direction of the question, defined by the ordering of the entities in the source text.

##### Tasks

Each fact is represented as a relation between a subject and an object entity, where the subject precedes the object in the source text. The left context consists of all text preceding the object entity.

We use two tasks to measure encoding:

* nosep, leftmargin=\*

  Completion: Proposition completion of the left context.
* nosep, leftmargin=\*

  Contextual: A high-verbatim question posed over the same left context.

Completion can be ambiguous for post-trained LLMs, which may generate plausible continuations without stating the target fact. The contextual question explicitly elicits the fact while preserving the original context, mitigating this issue.

We use four tasks to measure knowledge:

* nosep, leftmargin=\*

  Direct: A closed-book question, whose answer is the object entity.
* nosep, leftmargin=\*

  Direct Natural: A conversational rephrasing of the Direct question.
* nosep, leftmargin=\*

  Reverse: An inverted question where the object becomes the subject.
* nosep, leftmargin=\*

  Reverse Natural: A conversational rephrasing of the Reverse question.

Finally, we reformulate each of the four closed-book questions as multiple-choice questions with four answer options. The distractors are designed to be plausible and realistic. These tasks evaluate sensitivity to task format and, crucially, the ability to verify facts. This shift from generation to verification probes a distinct form of factual knowledge.

#### A.2 Fact Extraction Process

We aim to extract facts that are likely encoded by LLMs (to enable measurement of encoding even for small LLMs) yet remain non-trivial. Notably, we intentionally avoid extracting facts in a schema-based triplet format (subject, relation, object). Our goal is to generate non-trivial questions that are not limited to simple structural relations and may involve more complex ones (e.g., “What is the condition that caused Erika Nordby to spend two hours without a heartbeat before she was revived?” ).

#### A.3 Question Generation Process

We use three stages of generation, refinement, and filtering to ensure that questions satisfy the required properties. The refinement stage resolves ambiguity and enforces specificity, for example, by clarifying the required answer type and adding minimal disambiguating context when needed. The filtering stage is grounded in Google Search to recall plausible alternative answers and to discard questions that admit multiple answers.

We first apply these three stages to construct the Direct question. Based on the Direct question, we generate the Reverse question, which is then refined and filtered. From the Direct question, we also generate the Direct Natural and Contextual questions, which are subsequently filtered. Similarly, we generate and filter the Reverse Natural questions. Finally, we generate the multiple-choice variants. Below, we describe the three-stage process used to construct the Direct question, and then elaborate on the remaining types.

##### Generation

The Direct questions are generated by instructing the LLM to design a question based on the left context such that the only correct answer is the object entity. The question uses only the information necessary for disambiguation ( minimal context ) while preserving exact phrasing from the source text ( high verbatim ). We also specify a set of requirements in the prompt (Box [E.2](https://arxiv.org/html/.SS2 "E.2 Question Generation Prompts ‣ Appendix E Prompts ‣ Appendix ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") ), including ensuring that the question is self-contained and does not include pronouns. We then refine the Direct questions using two additional prompts: one to improve specificity and one to enforce minimalism.

##### Refinement

The specification prompt (Box [E.2](https://arxiv.org/html/.SS2 "E.2 Question Generation Prompts ‣ Appendix E Prompts ‣ Appendix ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") ) instructs the LLM to evaluate a question’s specificity: whether it clearly defines the required level of detail in the answer, and to revise it if necessary. The LLM should recommend rejecting a question if it cannot be fixed, either because revising it would leak a non-trivial clue that makes the question too easy or because the question is too vague and would require a full rewrite. This stage rejected 45 questions, leaving Direct questions for 4955 facts. The minimalism prompt (Box [E.2](https://arxiv.org/html/.SS2 "E.2 Question Generation Prompts ‣ Appendix E Prompts ‣ Appendix ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") ) instructs the LLM to revise a question if it contains non-essential information that can be removed while preserving disambiguation and specificity.

##### Filtering

Some questions may still be vague or ambiguous, or admit multiple answers, according to sources outside the Wikipedia context. To filter these cases, we use Gemini-2.5-Pro with thinking grounded in Google Search. Each question is answered with search grounding, and a prompted LLM (Box [E.2](https://arxiv.org/html/.SS2 "E.2 Question Generation Prompts ‣ Appendix E Prompts ‣ Appendix ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") ) evaluates whether the question is suitable. If the grounded answer requests clarification, provides multiple possible answers, or varies by interpretation, the question is rejected. This process removes 10.6% of Direct questions. Filtering applies to all five question types: once a fact is discarded due to any of its questions, it is not reconsidered. The number of removed questions is reported in the relevant paragraphs below.

#### A.4 Manual Quality Validation

To ensure high data quality, we manually validate questions by adapting the protocol of Nahum et al. (2024) , which prioritizes instances in which an LLM ensemble fails. Specifically, we manually examine a fact and its ten associated questions if the four frontier LLMs (Gemini-3-Pro/Flash, GPT-5, and GPT-4.1; evaluated with and without thinking) unanimously fail to correctly answer at least one of the questions, i.e., $g(q)\leq 0.5$ . This process flagged 237 facts (10.7%). Upon manual review, we identified 43 ( $<$ 2%) facts and their associated questions as low quality, and they were subsequently removed from the benchmark. To further validate our data, we examined a subset of 50 facts in which three of the four models provided incorrect answers. We found only 3 errors in this subset. Together, these results suggest that low-quality questions are uncommon in the remaining data and that the benchmark meets a high standard.

### Appendix B Thinking and Recall

In § [5\.3](https://arxiv.org/html/.SS3 "5.3 Thinking as a Recovery Mechanism ‣ 5 Results ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") , we argued that thinking primarily facilitates recall of encoded knowledge. Here we present the full analysis, including formal definitions, detailed results across all models, and a discussion of limitations.

#### B.1 Three Possible Mechanisms

We consider three mechanisms by which thinking may improve factual performance on our benchmark. Each mechanism makes distinct empirical predictions, which we evaluate in the subsequent sections.

(i) Response diversity. Thinking may alter the output distribution by increasing its variance. With higher variance, the probability that at least one of $n$ samples is correct increases – a sampling effect analogous to the relationship between response diversity and $\text{pass}@k$ ( Wang et al., 2023 ; Yang et al., 2025 ) . Importantly, this mechanism does not reflect improved access to knowledge; rather, it is a statistical consequence of broader exploration of the output space.

_Example:_ Without thinking, a model asked “Where did Oasis play their first show?” might produce the same incorrect answer in 8 out of 8 samples. Thinking trajectories, by incorporating additional sampling steps, may lead to additional answers, some correct, most not, yielding, say, 2 out of 8 correct samples. Availability of the correct answer has improved (from 0 to 2 correct), but robustness has not: the model does not reliably converge to the correct answer.

_Prediction:_ Response diversity increases _availability_ (the chance of at least one correct answer across samples) but cannot increase _robustness_ (consistent correctness), since higher variance cannot produce reliable convergence to the same correct output.

(ii) Inference. Thinking may enable multi-step derivation from other encoded facts. By chaining together related knowledge, the LLM can produce an answer that it does not store directly. This mechanism relies on the availability of related encoded premises, not on the encoding status of the target fact itself.

_Example:_ A model may not encode that Oasis played their first gig at the Boardwalk. However, through thinking, it might chain together other related encoded facts: “Oasis formed in Manchester” $\rightarrow$ “the Boardwalk was a famous music club in Manchester during the 1990s” $\rightarrow$ “the Boardwalk hosted early gigs by emerging bands.” This inference produces the correct answer without direct recall.

_Prediction:_ Inference gains are regardless of whether the specific target fact is encoded, since it operates over _other related_ encoded facts. We therefore expect inference to produce correct answers even for non-encoded facts.

(iii) Recall facilitation. Thinking may help the model locate and recall a fact that is encoded but not accessible via direct generation. The fact is stored in the model’s parameters, as verified by the encoding tasks, but the bare question fails to activate its recall. Thinking provides additional computation that bridges this gap.

_Example:_ An LLM can complete the relevant Wikipedia proposition about Oasis’s first gig when given the left context. However, when asked “Where did Oasis play their first show?” without context, direct generation fails. With thinking, the model can generate intermediate context (e.g., recalling details about the band’s early history in Manchester) and perform additional computations that prime recall of the stored answer.

_Prediction:_ Recall facilitation, by definition, requires the target fact to be encoded. It should therefore benefit only encoded facts. Unlike response diversity, it can improve both availability and robustness, since successful recall of a stored fact yields consistent correctness.

#### B.2 Measuring Availability and Robustness

To operationalize the distinction between availability and robustness, we propose $\text{correct}\!\geq\!p$ , a metric that captures both aspects along a continuous spectrum. It is defined as the proportion of questions whose estimated probability of correctness across samples is at least $p$

For each question, we draw $n$ independent samples and observe $k$ correct responses, yielding an empirical correctness rate $\hat{p}=k/n$ . We then estimate the probability that, in a repeated run of $n$ samples, at least a $p$ fraction of the answers would be correct. Let $m=\lceil pn\rceil$ . Using a binomial model $K\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(n,\hat{p})$ , then:

| |$\hat{P}\!\left(K\geq m\right)\;=\;\sum_{j=m}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\,\hat{p}^{\,j}\,(1-\hat{p})^{\,n-j}.$ | |
| --- | --- | --- |

Aggregating over questions, $\text{correct}\!\geq\!p$ measures the fraction of questions for which this estimated probability exceeds 0.5, i.e., $\hat{P}\!\left(K\geq\lceil pn\rceil\right)>0.5$ .

The metric makes the trade-off between availability and robustness clear. At low values of $p$ (e.g., $p=0.125$ , corresponding to at least 1 correct out of 8), the metric asks whether the model can produce any correct answer, which is analogous to $\text{pass}@k$ . At high values of $p$ (e.g., $p=0.875$ ), it asks whether the model is correct almost all of the time. ⁷ ⁷ 7 With $n\!=\!8$ samples, $\text{correct}\!\geq\!p$ is insensitive to changes at very low correctness probabilities (roughly $p\!\lesssim\!0.1$ ), since such cases frequently yield zero correct samples. Thus, availability gains in the extreme low-probability tail may be underestimated, while robustness at high $p$ remains well-estimated.

#### B.5 The Role of Thinking-Specific Training

Our evaluation pools two distinct implementations of thinking: _native thinking_ , where the model was optimized during training to use thinking tokens (Gemini-3, Gemini-2.5, GPT-5 families), and _CoT prompting_ , where thinking is elicited at test time without dedicated training (Gemma3, GPT-4.1). The results suggest that these implementations yield qualitatively different effects. Native thinking consistently improves both availability and robustness, consistent with recall facilitation. CoT prompting produces mixed results: for Gemma3 models, it improves availability but degrades robustness, consistent with a response-diversity effect; for GPT-4.1, it yields modest gains in both.

This distinction has practical implications. The recall facilitation mechanism, in which thinking helps the model access encoded knowledge, may depend on thinking-specific training rather than merely on producing additional tokens at inference time. If so, the benefits of thinking for factuality are not a free lunch obtainable through prompting alone; they require dedicated training investment. We note, however, that our experimental design cannot fully disentangle the effects of thinking-specific training from other differences between model families (e.g., model size, data, post-training procedures). The pattern is suggestive but not conclusive.

#### D.1 Handling Partially and Other Grades

The question grade is computed as accuracy over CORRECT and INCORRECT responses only, excluding responses labeled PARTIALLY or OTHER. For example, if a question receives 3 CORRECT, 1 INCORRECT, 2 PARTIALLY, and 2 OTHER responses, its grade is $\tfrac{3}{3+1}=0.75$ . We exclude PARTIALLY and OTHER from grading because assigning them weights is subjective ( Kalai et al., 2025 ) .

We next describe how we handle PARTIALLY and OTHER responses at the fact level. We evaluated three strategies; Table [2](https://arxiv.org/html/.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Appendix D Methodological and Design Choices ‣ Appendix ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") reports the resulting profile distributions and the fraction of excluded facts.

The first strategy excludes a fact if any of its six profiling questions is non-gradable, meaning all responses are PARTIALLY or OTHER. This approach removes a substantial number of facts, around 10% for frontier LLMs and over 20% for Gemma3 models.

The second strategy groups questions into task pairs (encoding, direct, and reverse) and excludes a fact only if an entire pair is non-gradable. This substantially reduces exclusions to 1–4% for nearly all LLMs, while producing profile distributions nearly identical to the first strategy.

The third strategy extends the second by assigning PARTIALLY responses a weight of 0.51, allowing facts to be considered known even when all responses are PARTIALLY. While this further reduces exclusions to below 1% and yields results very similar to the second strategy, it introduces subjective judgment that is harder to justify. We therefore adopt the second strategy throughout the paper.

Finally, in § [2\.2](https://arxiv.org/html/.SS2 "2.2 Knowledge Profiles ‣ 2 Knowledge Profiling ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") , we do not define a profile for facts that are not encoded but are known without thinking. Such cases are rare (less than 0.5% across all models; see the “Direct Inference” column in Table [2](https://arxiv.org/html/.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Appendix D Methodological and Design Choices ‣ Appendix ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") ) and are likely due to sampling noise or grader error; excluding this profile does not change the results.

#### D.2 Threshold Selection

Recall that both our definitions of _encodes_ and _knows_ rely on a threshold $g(q)>\tau$ . A natural choice is $\tau=0.5$ , which corresponds to correctness being more likely than incorrectness. However, the threshold is a design choice, and different values can yield different profile distributions.

To assess sensitivity to this choice, Figure [18](https://arxiv.org/html/.F18 "Figure 18 ‣ D.4 Multiple Response Generation ‣ Appendix D Methodological and Design Choices ‣ Appendix ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") presents profile distributions for five additional reasonable values of $\tau$ . While the exact proportions of profiles vary with $\tau$ , our main conclusions are robust. Specifically, (1) encoding is nearly saturated for frontier LLMs, even under a conservative threshold of $\tau=0.99$ ; (2) recall failures account for a substantial fraction of errors, and their relative impact increases under stricter thresholds; and (3) thinking consistently acts as a recovery mechanism, with over 10% of facts known only with thinking across all threshold choices.

#### D.3 Grader Evaluation

Our evaluation relies on a prompted LLM grader, a common practice in short-form factuality evaluation ( Wei et al., 2024a ; Haas et al., 2025 ) . We use Gemini-2.5-Pro with thinking as the primary grader. The prompt is adapted from SimpleQA ( Wei et al., 2024a ) and refined through manual error analysis with Gemini-2.5-Pro. In this subsection, we assess robustness to the choice of the grader’s underlying LLM by comparing our grader to a GPT-5-based grader with thinking (medium effort). We uniformly sample 4,160 responses across six tasks and all evaluated LLMs. The two graders agree on 98.2% of responses.

#### D.4 Multiple Response Generation

For each task, we generate $n\!=\!8$ responses per question for three reasons. First, our goal is to assess factual knowledge at the level of facts; a single response is a noisy estimator and may misclassify a fact as not encoded or not known due to sampling variance. Second, because our questions are not single-token predictions, probability-based estimates are not well defined, and token-level likelihoods are inaccessible for most proprietary LLMs, making repeated sampling a natural alternative. Finally, multiple responses are required to distinguish availability (at least one correct response) from robustness (all responses correct), which is part of our analysis of inference-time thinking.

To assess the effect of multiple sampling and justify our choice of $n\!=\!8$ , we analyze how the fraction of facts with $g(q)\!>\!0.5$ varies with $n$ across four tasks: two encoding tasks (completion and contextual) and two knowledge tasks (direct and reverse). For each task and model, we perform 1,000 bootstrap resamples, each time subsampling $n$ responses per question and recomputing the fraction of facts with $g(q)\!>\!0.5$ . Figure [19](https://arxiv.org/html/.F19 "Figure 19 ‣ D.4 Multiple Response Generation ‣ Appendix D Methodological and Design Choices ‣ Appendix ‣ Empty Shelves or Lost Keys? Recall Is the Bottleneck for Parametric Factuality") shows the width of the 90% empirical bootstrap confidence interval (95th–5th percentiles) as a function of $n$ . Across the evaluated models, the interval width falls below 1% for all tasks at $n\!=\!8$ , indicating that the estimated fraction is stable to within $\pm$ 0\.5%. We therefore use $n\!=\!8$ throughout the paper.
